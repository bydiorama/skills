# Chapter 5 -- Copywriting Style and Tone of the Guidelines Themselves

> Meta-analysis of 180 brand guideline documents (36 Diorama, 144 external).
> Focus: how the guideline documents communicate their rules -- the writing approach of the manuals, not the brands' own tone of voice.
> Data extracted from per-document analysis files, April 2026.

---

## 1. Writing Approaches

Brand guideline documents fall into three broad writing styles based on how they communicate identity rules to their readers. Classification was based on the dominant register across each document, not isolated passages.

### Category definitions

| Style | Description | Identifying signals |
|-------|-------------|---------------------|
| **Instructional / Technical** | Engineering-grade specifications. Rules expressed as measurements, ratios, and precise constraints. Minimal narrative; assumes the reader is a production professional. | Grid coordinates, millimeter dimensions, mathematical formulas, numbered specification sections, "shall/must" language |
| **Inspirational / Narrative** | Story-driven. Opens with brand philosophy, justifies rules through narrative rationale, and aims to make the reader *feel* the brand before executing it. | Opening manifestos, "why" before "what," emotive adjectives, brand personality woven into instruction |
| **Hybrid** | Combines both. Typically opens sections with a narrative introduction explaining purpose, then transitions to precise specifications. The dominant modern approach. | Section intros with contextual framing followed by spec tables, alternating between "here is why" and "here is how" |

### Distribution across 180 documents

| Style | Count | % | Avg. quality score |
|-------|------:|------:|---:|
| Instructional / Technical | 55 | 30.6% | 3.4 |
| Inspirational / Narrative | 33 | 18.3% | 3.2 |
| Hybrid | 92 | 51.1% | 3.9 |

**Key findings:**

- The **hybrid approach is dominant** (51.2%) and correlates with the highest average quality scores. This is not surprising: the best guidelines explain *why* a rule exists before specifying *how* to follow it. Documents that do both consistently score higher.
- **Pure instructional** documents (30.6%) are the second-largest group. They cluster in two distinct populations: pre-2000 corporate identity manuals (NASA, Bell System, Dreher, Expo 67) where instructional was the only mode, and modern partner/compliance guides (Apple Channel Affiliates, Uber Quick Guide, Dropbox, Zoom) that deliberately strip narrative to focus on rules for third-party implementers. Network Rail (E084) exemplifies the modern engineering-register instructional approach, with formal engineering-precise language governing 8 color specification systems.
- **Pure inspirational** documents (18.2%) are the smallest group. These tend to be strategy-heavy brand books where the visual identity specifications are thin or deferred to separate documents. Fiat's Brazilian guide ("The guidelines contained here aim to inspire your creativity, not limit it") and SPACE10's principles-led philosophy deck are archetypes. They score lowest on average because inspiration without specification leaves implementers without actionable guidance.

### Diorama vs. external split

| Style | Diorama (n=36) | External (n=144) |
|-------|:-:|:-:|
| Instructional / Technical | 14 (38.9%) | 41 (28.5%) |
| Inspirational / Narrative | 4 (11.1%) | 29 (20.1%) |
| Hybrid | 18 (50.0%) | 74 (51.4%) |

Diorama documents skew slightly more instructional and less inspirational than the external corpus. This likely reflects the agency's Central European design tradition, which prioritizes systematic specification. The hybrid ratio is nearly identical, suggesting that the agency has adopted the modern best-practice approach at the same rate as the broader market.

## 2. Rule Communication

How do guidelines express their rules? The phrasing of restrictions reveals the document's underlying philosophy -- controlling versus enabling.

### Dominant rule-communication patterns

| Pattern | Description | Example | Prevalence |
|---------|-------------|---------|:---:|
| **Do / Don't** | Binary pairs with visual examples of correct and incorrect usage | Dropbox (3 of 5 chapters are explicitly "Don'ts"), Motorola, TikTok, American Express | ~45% |
| **Always / Never** | Absolute imperatives with no ambiguity | Bell System ("Under no circumstances is the bell symbol to be redrawn"), Apple ("Never use Apple logo standalone"), Kia (12 documented misuse rules) | ~30% |
| **Prefer / Avoid** | Softer guidance that acknowledges exceptions | Origameo ("contractions preferred"), Bumble ("one exclamation point max"), SPACE10 ("principles rather than hard rules") | ~15% |
| **Visual-only** | Rules communicated entirely through visual examples with minimal or no text | Arthaus (zero extractable text), Dreher (image-based), several Diorama documents with image-based PDFs | ~10% |

**Multiple patterns coexist within most documents.** A typical 50-page hybrid guideline will use "Always/Never" for logo rules, "Do/Don't" for color and typography, and "Prefer/Avoid" for photography and tone of voice. The strictness of language scales with the perceived risk of the element: logos get absolutes, photography gets suggestions.

### Correlation with quality

Documents that combine **positive examples with negative examples** score consistently higher than those using only one mode:

- **Do + Don't pairs** (showing both correct and incorrect): avg. 3.8/5
- **Don'ts only** (Dropbox model -- telling people what not to do without showing what to do): avg. 2.5/5
- **Do only** (affirmative guidance with no cautionary examples): avg. 3.1/5

The pattern is clear. The Titans TOV supplement (D019) explicitly notes the absence of "don'ts" as a gap: "The document does not include any 'don't' examples, anti-patterns, or forbidden language. It is entirely affirmative in its guidance." Meanwhile, Burger King's extensive do/don't examples for each voice characteristic score 4/5, and TikTok's "Things to Avoid" sections with direct Do/Don't language comparisons are praised as "exceptionally practical."

### The "Things to Avoid" reframe

Several modern documents (TikTok, Bumble, WeWork) have moved away from the blunt "Don't" label toward softer alternatives:

- "Things to Avoid" (TikTok)
- Avoidances (Bumble: "No bee imagery/puns; no 'shoot'/'shot' language")
- Sensitivity guidance framed as cultural awareness rather than prohibition

This reframing preserves the practical value of negative examples while sounding less authoritarian -- a meaningful tonal shift in how guidelines address their own audience.

## 3. Brand Voice IN the Document

A critical question: does the guideline document itself speak in the brand's voice, or does it adopt a neutral, consultant-register "meta-voice"?

### Three modes observed

| Mode | Description | Count | % |
|------|-------------|------:|---:|
| **Neutral / Consultant** | The document speaks about the brand in third person. Professional, descriptive, detached. | 103 | 57.2% |
| **Brand voice** | The document is written in the brand's own voice. First person ("we"), brand personality evident in the guideline prose. | 46 | 25.6% |
| **Minimal text** | Documents with so little prose that no voice is discernible -- pure specification or visual-only. | 31 | 17.2% |

### Examples of each

**Neutral / Consultant voice** (the majority):
- NASA (1976): Pure technical standards language. "The NASA Logotype is the primary identity mark."
- Bell System (1970): "Under no circumstances is the bell symbol to be redrawn, reproportioned or modified in any manner." Formal, authoritative, third-person.
- Barbican: "Precise, instructional, and design-literate."
- Kia: Mathematical precision, no personality in the guideline prose despite a strong brand personality.
- Aix-Marseille: "Guidelines framed as a practical tool ('not a constraint') to ensure visual coherence."

**Brand voice** (the document practices what it preaches):
- IKEA TOV (E092): The guide is "itself an exemplar of the IKEA voice -- simple, warm, conversational, with humor ('Yay, it's Monday!')." The document about IKEA's voice *sounds like IKEA*.
- Spotify (E100): "Hej hej" greeting. "Friendly, informal Scandinavian tone" in the guideline prose itself.
- Strava (E090): "The writing itself exemplifies the brand voice." (Scored 5/5.)
- Klarna (E053): "Resist the temptation to change aspects of the system with which your personal tastes may disagree." -- confident, direct, a little cheeky. Pure Klarna.
- Fiat Brazil (E030): "This is the new Fiat." Opens with the brand's own personality.
- Pleo (E101): Conversational throughout; "Pleo rhymes with Rio" on the cover page.
- Hometree (E122): Energy-particle color names (Photon, Electron, Joule, Watt, Kelvin) embed brand personality into the specification language itself.
- Virgin Media (E088): The MOJO tone of voice system permeates the document's own writing.
- Channel 4 (E025): Sharp, provocative example headlines ("Drug companies are making a killing") demonstrate the voice rather than merely describing it.

**Distinctive writing registers beyond the three modes** appear in several newly analyzed documents, expanding the observed range of guideline writing styles:

- **Educational / Historical:** Ogilvy Typography (E085) adopts a deeply educational writing style, grounding typographic rules in Johnston heritage and historical rationale. The document teaches as much as it instructs.
- **Satirical:** Christopher Doyle (E077) treats the brand guideline format itself as satire, adopting a human-as-brand conceit that subverts the conventions of the genre.
- **Art-discourse:** The Shed (E149) writes in the register of art criticism and institutional discourse, appropriate to a venue whose identity derives from a Lawrence Weiner artwork. At 120 pages and only 2.0 MB, the document is overwhelmingly text-driven.
- **Metaphor-structured:** Tramways Coffee (E083) uses coffee production stages (harvesting, roasting, brewing) as section metaphors, embedding the brand's domain expertise into the document's organizational structure itself.

**The brand-voice approach correlates with higher quality.** Documents where the guidelines themselves embody the brand voice average 4.1/5, versus 3.4/5 for neutral-voice documents. This is partly a selection effect (brands sophisticated enough to write in-voice tend to produce better guidelines overall), but it also reflects a genuine advantage: when the document demonstrates the voice, implementers absorb it through reading rather than having to decode abstract descriptors.

## 4. Section Introductions

Do chapters open with contextual framing explaining *why* a section matters, or do they jump straight to specifications?

### Three patterns

| Pattern | Description | Prevalence |
|---------|-------------|:---:|
| **Specs-first** | Section opens directly with rules, values, or measurements. No preamble. | ~40% |
| **Brief framing** | 1-3 sentences establishing context or purpose before diving into specs. | ~35% |
| **Narrative introduction** | A paragraph or more explaining the rationale, philosophy, or creative thinking behind the section. | ~25% |

**Specs-first** dominates older documents and technical manuals. NASA's typography section opens with "Sans Serif: Helvetica" -- no explanation of why Helvetica was chosen. Bell System begins with reproduction specifications. Most Diorama documents in the D001-D015 range follow this pattern: logo construction grids open the section with no contextual framing.

**Brief framing** is the modern default. Examples:
- Uber: "Uber Move -- proprietary brand typeface, described as 'key element in our brand.'" One sentence of context, then specifications.
- Docusign: "DS Indigo -- custom bespoke typeface. 'Happy medium between classic grotesque and geometric sans.'" A positioning sentence before the weight matrix.
- Finland: "Finlandica -- designed specifically for the Finland Brand Identity, open source/free to use." Context about provenance and licensing before detailed specs.

**Narrative introduction** appears in the highest-scoring documents:
- Whitney Museum (E059, 5/5): A full "Introduction" section spanning pages 15-24, covering "Unfolding the line, The Responsive W, The W-shaped grid, Flexibility and inflexibility, Walls and words, Authorship."
- OpenAI (E105, 4/5): Brand values are "explicitly rooted in the mission and intended to 'guide future design explorations.'"
- Bumble (E061, 4/5): Voice spectrum mapped against customer journey stages -- the *why* precedes every *what*.
- A Punt (E018, 4/5): Every design decision justified through Valencian cultural references (paella, oranges, Mediterranean, ceramic traditions).
- Corvus Atrium (D025): Brand built around the "Hniezdo" (Nest) metaphor. "Different bird species build different nests, just as different people need different homes at different life stages." The metaphor frames every subsequent design choice.

**The presence of "why" introductions is the single strongest textual predictor of quality scores above 4/5.** Documents that explain their reasoning produce more consistent implementation -- not because the specs are better, but because implementers who understand intent make better judgment calls at the edges.

## 5. Language Patterns

### Common verbs in rule communication

Analyzed across the corpus, the most frequently used verbs in guideline prose cluster into four families:

| Family | Verbs | Tone |
|--------|-------|------|
| **Prohibition** | do not, never, avoid, must not, shall not, prohibited | Authoritative, restrictive |
| **Obligation** | must, always, ensure, shall, required, maintain | Directive, non-negotiable |
| **Recommendation** | should, prefer, recommend, suggested, ideally | Advisory, flexible |
| **Description** | is, uses, functions as, represents, conveys | Neutral, explanatory |

Older documents (pre-2000) lean heavily on Prohibition and Obligation verbs. Modern documents (post-2015) increasingly use Description verbs -- explaining what the system *does* rather than commanding what the reader *must do*. This reflects a broader shift from brand-guidelines-as-law to brand-guidelines-as-toolkit.

### Sentence structure

Three dominant patterns emerge:

1. **Imperative + Specification**: "Use Futura Bold for all headlines." / "Set leading to 120% of point size." Direct, efficient, unambiguous. The workhorse sentence of brand guidelines.

2. **Condition + Rule**: "When the logo appears on dark backgrounds, use the white version." / "At sizes below 20mm, switch to the simplified mark." Handles contextual variation gracefully.

3. **Rationale + Rule**: "The clear space preserves balance and wholeness. No other graphic elements should appear within the clearance area." (OpenAI) The premium construction -- gives the reader both the *why* and the *what*.

### Level of specificity

Specificity varies enormously across the corpus:

- **Hyper-specific** (engineering grade): Kia's graphic motif placement rules use percentage margins, anchor point ranges, and radius equations. Bell System provides engineering drawings from AT&T Engineering Manager-Buildings. Dreher includes millimeter-accurate vehicle livery placement for each Fiat model.
- **Moderately specific** (production-ready): Bumble defines margin ratios per format category (Vertical 9:10, Square 1:1, Horizontal 10:9). Docusign provides tracking values per scale (-2% display, -1% subheadlines, 0% body).
- **Principle-level** (directional): SPACE10 offers guiding principles rather than hard rules. Tate provides a custom typeface with 8 styles but "zero prescriptive hierarchy rules."

**The production-ready middle ground correlates with the highest quality scores and broadest usability.** Hyper-specific documents are invaluable for large-scale rollouts but brittle in novel contexts. Principle-level documents empower experienced designers but leave junior implementers without guardrails.

## 6. Bilingual and Multilingual Documents

### Non-English documents in the corpus

| Language | Documents | Examples |
|----------|----------:|---------|
| Slovak | 11 | D002, D009, D013, D015, D026, D033 (Noveba), D008 (Bistro), and others |
| Catalan/Valencian | 1 | E018 (A Punt) |
| Spanish | 1 | E021 (Arthaus) |
| Brazilian Portuguese | 1 | E030 (Fiat Brazil) |
| French | 1 | E154 (Aix-Marseille) |
| Italian | 1 | E129 (Dreher) |
| Bilingual FR/EN | 1 | E049 (Expo 67) |
| **Total non-English** | **17** | **10.0% of corpus** |

An additional ~8 documents use English as the primary guideline language but include significant non-English content in application examples (D001 with Slovak event details, D003 with Czech pangrams, D023 with Slovak example copy, D025 with Slovak brand story alongside English instructions, D028 with German market brochures).

### How non-English documents handle terminology

Three approaches were observed:

1. **Full native language** (most Slovak documents, Aix-Marseille, Arthaus): All section headings, instructions, and terminology in the local language. Design terms are often borrowed from English even within native-language text ("logo," "branding," "font," "layout" appear unchanged). This suggests design terminology has become a de facto international vocabulary.

2. **Bilingual structure** (Expo 67, A Punt): Content presented in two languages side by side or in parallel sections. Expo 67 follows Canadian federal language policy (French then English). A Punt's Valencian text reinforces the public broadcaster's linguistic mission.

3. **Hybrid language** (Corvus Atrium D025, Prosight D023): English for guideline structure and instructions, local language for brand copy and marketing examples. This is the most practical approach for international agencies producing guidelines for local-market brands.

### Implications

- Non-English guidelines present a unique challenge for AI-generated systems. Design terminology is largely English-universal, but brand narrative, tone of voice examples, and cultural rationale require native-language fluency.
- The Diorama corpus is heavily Slovak-influenced. Approximately 30% of Diorama documents use Slovak as the primary or significant secondary language. An AI toolkit serving this agency needs Slovak language capability for brand story sections, even if technical specifications default to English.
- The REMPO analysis (D009) notes: "Entire manual is in Slovak, not English -- somewhat unusual for a brand manual in this format." This suggests even within the Slovak market, English is the expected default for brand guidelines, and Slovak-language documents are a deliberate local-market choice.

## 7. Toolkit Implications -- Recommended Writing Style for AI-Generated Guidelines

Based on the patterns observed across 180 documents, the following recommendations emerge for the AI-generated guidelines toolkit:

### Primary recommendation: Hybrid approach with brief framing

The hybrid style (narrative context + precise specification) scored highest across the corpus (avg. 3.9/5) and should be the default output mode. Each section should open with 1-3 sentences of contextual framing before transitioning to specifications.

**Template pattern:**

> *[Brief rationale: why this element matters to the brand]*
>
> *[Precise specification: values, rules, measurements]*
>
> *[Do/Don't pair: one correct example, one incorrect example]*

### Specific writing rules

1. **Use the brand's voice in the document.** Documents that embody the brand voice score 0.7 points higher on average. The AI should generate guideline prose that reflects the brand personality being documented -- formal brands get formal guidelines, playful brands get playful guidelines.

2. **Pair every Don't with a Do.** The data is unambiguous: documents with paired positive/negative examples outperform those with only one mode. Never present a "Don't" without a corresponding "Do."

3. **Lead with "why" for conceptual sections, lead with "what" for specification sections.** Brand strategy, color philosophy, and typography rationale benefit from narrative openings. Logo clearspace, color hex values, and minimum sizes should lead with the specification.

4. **Use conditional phrasing for contextual rules.** "When [context], use [rule]" handles real-world variation better than blanket imperatives. Brand guidelines that accommodate context (dark/light backgrounds, small/large sizes, digital/print) are more useful than those that do not.

5. **Scale imperative strength to element criticality:**
   - Logo: "Always / Never" (absolute)
   - Color: "Use / Do not use" (firm)
   - Typography: "Preferred / Avoid" (recommended)
   - Photography: "Aim for / Steer away from" (directional)

6. **Include a brief "why" for every non-obvious rule.** "Clear space equals the width of the 'U' in the wordmark" is a specification. "Clear space preserves the logo's visual integrity and ensures it is never crowded by surrounding elements" is a rationale. Include both.

7. **Default to English for structure, support local language for brand narrative.** The hybrid-language approach (D025 Corvus Atrium model) is the most practical: English for section headings, technical instructions, and universal specifications; local language for brand story, taglines, and tone of voice examples.

8. **Name things memorably.** Hometree's energy-particle color names (Photon, Electron, Joule), Bumble's voice spectrum (Sincere > Supportive > Optimistic > Bold > Witty), and Burger King's food-centric palette (Mayo Egg White, Crunchy Green, BBQ Brown) all score 4/5 or higher. Memorable naming makes guidelines stick. The AI should generate evocative, brand-aligned names for colors and design elements rather than defaulting to generic descriptors.

9. **Avoid the "inspire, not limit" paradox.** Fiat's opening promise ("The guidelines contained here aim to inspire your creativity, not limit it") sounds generous but can undermine the document's authority. The data shows that documents with clear, specific rules score higher than those that prioritize creative freedom. Inspiration and precision are not opposites -- the best documents (Whitney, Kia, Docusign, Burger King) are both inspiring *and* precise.

10. **Keep the meta-voice consistent.** Whatever register the document adopts -- warm, technical, playful, formal -- it should maintain that register throughout. The most jarring guideline documents are those that open with an inspirational manifesto and then shift to dry legal language for logo rules. Consistency of voice in the document itself signals consistency of the brand it represents.

---

## Summary

The copywriting style of brand guidelines has evolved from pure technical specification (1960s-1970s) through narrative-heavy brand books (2000s-2010s) to the current hybrid equilibrium where context and specification coexist. The highest-quality documents share three writing characteristics: they explain why before specifying what, they demonstrate the brand voice rather than merely describing it, and they pair positive examples with cautionary ones. For the AI toolkit, the hybrid approach with brief contextual framing, brand-voice-inflected prose, and scaled imperative strength represents the evidence-based optimal default.
