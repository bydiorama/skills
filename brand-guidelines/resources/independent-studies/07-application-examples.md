# Independent Study 7: Application Examples

> Analysis of application mockups and templates across 180 brand guideline documents (36 Diorama, 144 external).
> Corpus: `/Research/per-document/diorama/` and `/Research/per-document/external/`

---

## 1. Prevalence

Application examples are among the most widely included sections in brand guidelines. Unlike photography or tone of voice -- which many documents omit entirely -- the vast majority of documents show at least some form of the brand "in the wild."

| Coverage level | Count | % | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dedicated applications section with multiple touchpoints** | ~115 | 68% | Named section or chapter with mockups across 3+ application types (stationery, digital, signage, etc.) |
| **Partial / limited applications** | ~30 | 18% | One or two application types shown (often just business cards or a single poster), or applications embedded within other sections without a dedicated chapter |
| **Referenced but not shown** | ~12 | 7% | TOC lists applications or text refers to them, but pages were not included or content is placeholder |
| **No applications** | ~13 | 8% | No application examples of any kind -- document focuses purely on identity elements (logo, color, type) or is a strategy/TOV-only document |

### Correlation with quality score

Documents with dedicated, multi-touchpoint application sections average **3.9/5**, compared to **2.8/5** for those with no applications. The highest-scoring documents (5/5) almost universally include extensive application examples: HSBC (181 pages with stationery, literature, digital, events, app icons), F1 (205 pages across press, digital, environmental, merchandise), IOC (129 pages with print, digital, textile-grade specs), AB-InBev (192 pages with flex assets and applications spanning 65+ pages), Strava (101 pages with kits, campaigns, and the "Spandex" design system), and NJ Transit (169 pages covering every conceivable vehicle model and signage type).

Application examples are not merely decorative -- they function as proof that the identity system works in practice. Documents that stop at logo, color, and type without showing applications consistently score lower, because they leave implementors guessing about context, scale, and real-world behavior.

### Diorama vs. External

| | Diorama (36) | External (134) |
|---|---|---|
| Dedicated section (3+ types) | 28 (78%) | 87 (65%) |
| Partial / limited | 4 (11%) | 26 (19%) |
| None or referenced only | 4 (11%) | 21 (16%) |

Diorama documents are significantly more likely to include dedicated application sections than the external corpus. This reflects the agency's delivery model where application mockups are a standard deliverable, not an optional add-on. Documents like D017-Vratna (60 pages of applications across ski passes, menus, vehicle wraps, wayfinding, OOH, social media, and merchandise), D016-CORWIN (50+ pages of applications), and D033-NOVEBA (82 pages including OOH, print, digital, and construction-site merch) set a high bar for application coverage.

## 3. Digital vs. Print Emphasis

### The shift is real but incomplete

The corpus spans documents from 1976 (NASA) to 2025 (New Museum), allowing a clear temporal reading. The balance has shifted decisively toward digital, but print has not disappeared -- it has been restructured.

**Pre-2010 documents** (NASA, Bell System, Expo67, Ferrari, Dreher, Deutsche Bank, Apple Vintage, Channel Five, US Army Corps, Sony VAIO): Applications are overwhelmingly print-focused. Stationery (letterhead, envelopes, business cards), signage, vehicle livery, and packaging dominate. Digital is absent or limited to a single "website" page. The Ferrari manual (168 pages) is entirely dedicated to stationery and logo reproduction.

**2010--2017 documents** (Queens 2015, Barbican, Origameo, Ogilvy, Channel 4, RAC, Habitat): Print still leads but digital sections begin appearing as distinct chapters. Social media mockups emerge, initially focused on Facebook (the Queens 2015 guidelines include extensive Facebook-specific templates with the then-current 20% text rule, but no Instagram or Twitter). Email signatures get their own pages. Websites move from "a page showing a laptop" to actual layout specifications.

**2018--2025 documents** (Bumble, Docusign, Tupperware, Hulu, Strava, Bolt, Pleo, New Museum, Research Ireland): Digital applications now typically outnumber print. Social media gets multiple pages (Instagram Stories, Reels, LinkedIn, carousels). Product/UI screenshots appear for SaaS brands. Motion and video templates become standard. However, business cards, letterhead, and folders persist in nearly every comprehensive guide. The physical has not been replaced -- it has been deprioritized to a smaller, standard set.

### The current equilibrium

In documents from 2020 onward, a typical split is:

- **60--70% digital**: Social media templates, website layouts, digital ads, email signatures, app icons, presentation templates, video/motion
- **30--40% print**: Business cards, letterhead, one envelope format, folders, posters/OOH, and select merchandise

The most notable shift is not from print to digital but from **stationery to social media** as the anchor application category. In pre-2015 documents, the stationery set (business cards + letterhead + envelopes + folders) was the de facto "proof of concept" for the brand. In post-2020 documents, that role has moved to social media templates and presentation decks.

## 4. Social Media

### Platform coverage

Social media applications appear in ~78 documents (46%). The platforms covered, ranked by frequency:

| Platform | ~Docs | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Instagram (feed + stories)** | ~55 | The dominant platform. Feed posts, Stories, Reels, Highlights covers. Often shown as phone mockups |
| **Facebook** | ~30 | Cover images, post templates, ad formats. Declining -- virtually absent in post-2022 documents |
| **LinkedIn** | ~20 | Profile pages, post templates. Growing, especially in B2B/corporate brands |
| **YouTube** | ~12 | Channel banners, profile pictures, thumbnail templates |
| **Twitter/X** | ~8 | Tweet treatments, profile images. Twitter's own guidelines (E045, E052) obviously cover this; few others do |
| **TikTok** | ~3 | Very rare in brand guidelines despite platform dominance. TikTok's own B2B guide (E016) is the primary example |

### Templates vs. guidelines

Documents take two distinct approaches to social media:

1. **Template-driven** (~40 docs): Provide specific, production-ready templates with exact dimensions, type placement, color zones, and content frameworks. Examples: Queens 2023 (D014) with Instagram Story variants, NOVEBA (D033) with Instagram feed/stories/LinkedIn/YouTube, CORWIN (D016) with a grid of post templates, Bumble (E061) with format-specific grid systems, KALW (E118) with complexity scales from simple to complex.

2. **Guideline-driven** (~20 docs): Provide principles and rules rather than fixed templates. Examples: Peloton (E038) with pre-written copy variants for partner social posts, TikTok (E016) with do/don't examples for advertising, SHARK (D002) with principles about experience-focused content. This approach is more common in partner/external-facing guides where the brand cannot control execution directly.

The template-driven approach is overwhelmingly more common and more practical. Documents that provide both -- templates plus principles for adapting them -- score highest for usability.

### The Instagram dominance

Instagram has become the default "proof of concept" platform for social media applications, replacing Facebook's prior dominance. In the Diorama portfolio specifically, Instagram mockups appear in virtually every project from 2019 onward (Rajska, Queens 2023, NOVEBA, NORDIKA, Vratna, BJD). The standard deliverable includes:

- Feed post templates (square, 1080x1080)
- Story templates (vertical, 1080x1920)
- Profile picture specification
- Occasionally: Reels format, Highlights covers, carousel templates

LinkedIn is emerging as the secondary platform, particularly for B2B and corporate brands (Howden, HCMA, Origameo). YouTube channel branding appears occasionally but is rarely given detailed template treatment.

## 5. Stationery

### The traditional suite

The "stationery set" -- business cards, letterhead, envelopes, and folders -- remains the most entrenched application category. Business cards alone appear in 56% of all documents, making them the single most common application mockup.

### Is stationery declining?

Yes, but slowly and unevenly.

**Fully present (traditional stationery suite):** ~60 docs (35%) include business cards + letterhead + envelopes as a unified set. This is standard in Diorama documents (D003-FONA, D006-DEUS, D008-Marina Dorcol, D013-Rajska, D016-CORWIN, D017-Vratna, D033-NOVEBA, D034-Queens 2015), corporate brands (HSBC, Ogilvy, Ferrari, MICA, ASICS), and institutional brands (NMAAHC, NY Botanical Garden, Crafts Council).

**Reduced to business cards only:** ~25 docs (15%) include business cards but omit letterhead and envelopes. This is increasingly common in tech and digital-native brands (Bolt, Zoom, Handshake, Snapchat).

**Omitted entirely:** ~35 docs (21%) include no stationery at all. These fall into two categories: (a) minimal external/partner guides that focus only on logo usage (Twitter, Uber, Snapchat, Zip), and (b) strategy-heavy or TOV-only documents (IKEA TOV, Nike Circularity, Fiat).

**The remaining ~50 docs (29%)** include stationery elements selectively -- perhaps letterhead but no envelope, or a folder but no business cards.

### The shift within stationery

The internal composition of stationery sections has changed:

- **Envelopes**: Clearly declining. Many modern documents skip envelopes entirely. When they appear, they are typically a single DL format.
- **Folders**: Stable. The A4 document folder persists because physical meeting collateral remains common in real estate, finance, and corporate contexts.
- **Letterhead**: Declining in page count but not in inclusion rate. Where pre-2010 documents might devote 3--4 pages to letterhead (first page, continuation sheet, fax cover sheet), modern documents show a single page.
- **Business cards**: Stable. The most resilient stationery item. Even digital-first brands tend to include business card specs. The format has evolved toward more creative treatments (vertical orientation, colored stock, embossing specs) rather than disappearing.
- **Email signatures**: Rising and partially replacing letterhead as the "daily communication" application. Email signatures now appear in ~24% of documents and are often specified to pixel-level precision (FONA, SHARK, HSBC, ASICS, Aludium).

## 6. Environmental / Signage

### Prevalence

Environmental and signage applications appear in ~32 documents (19%). This is lower than stationery or social media because signage is highly industry-dependent.

### Which industries lead?

| Industry | Signage focus | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Real estate / property** | Wayfinding, building identification, construction hoardings | Rajska (D013), NORDIKA (D018), CORWIN (D016), Corvus Atrium (D025), DSTRCT Berlin (D007/D011) |
| **Transit / infrastructure** | Station signage, vehicle graphics, directional systems | NJ Transit (E065 -- the most comprehensive signage system in the corpus), Network Rail (E084 -- station fascia/totems/projecting signs, house flags, 3D fabricated symbols, dot-matrix indicators, vector assets in EPS/SVG), in.kanal (D031) |
| **Museums / cultural** | Exhibition signage, gallery wayfinding, environmental identity | British Museum (E098), DIA (E023), Ogden Museum (E158), NMAAHC (E159), NY Botanical Garden (E151) |
| **Hospitality / resorts** | On-site navigation, facility identification | Vratna (D017 -- bilingual SK/EN wayfinding with distance markers), Habitat (E120) |
| **Broadcast / media** | On-site/studio signage, event environments | Channel 4 (E025), A Punt (E018), NatGeo (E104) |
| **Automotive** | Dealership/showroom signage, pictograms | Kia (E069 -- 25 custom pictograms matching logo aesthetic) |
| **Food / retail** | Store environments, POS systems | Burger King (E135), IceLand (E109), Habitat (E120) |

### Depth of coverage

Signage sections range enormously in depth:

- **Light**: A single page showing a building sign mockup (BONET SLEEK, GameJam)
- **Moderate**: 3--5 pages with wayfinding principles and a few sign types (Rajska, NORDIKA, British Museum)
- **Comprehensive**: 10--30+ pages with modular sign systems, material specifications, mounting details, distance/legibility formulas. NJ Transit (E065) is the gold standard -- specifying aluminum panel substrates, reflective sheeting, decal materials, and a 1" cap height per 50 feet viewing distance formula. Network Rail (E084) specifies station signage across fascia, totems, and projecting signs with 3D fabrication guidelines and dot-matrix indicator variants at 5 sizes down to 8x8px. NY Botanical Garden (E151) specifies Plexiglas dimensions and wood post cross-sections. Dreher (E129) includes millimeter-precise vehicle livery drawings for specific Fiat models.

### Exhibition / trade show

Exhibition and trade show applications appear in ~15 documents, primarily in B2B, event, and cultural contexts:

- FONA (D003): Event stand and roll-up banner
- ICFF (E143): Extremely detailed on-site signage specs (30' x 12' concourse banners, honeycomb board floor signs, steel plate stands)
- IBM Garage (E063): Physical space extensions including scaffolding, wallpaper, architectural elements
- Havas (E086): Lobby, business cards, letterhead, keycards, presentations, email, social, screensavers, 3D environmental applications with local cultural adaptation
- Brickline (E062): Spatial applications including brick inlay, bridge perforation, seating, and pavilion

## 7. Merchandise

### Prevalence

Branded merchandise appears in ~38 documents (22%). The most common merchandise items, ranked:

| Item | ~Docs | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **T-shirts** | ~28 | The anchor merchandise item. Often shown in 2--3 colorways |
| **Tote bags / canvas bags** | ~20 | Ubiquitous in sustainability-conscious and lifestyle brands |
| **Caps / beanies** | ~14 | Baseball caps most common; beanies in outdoor/winter brands |
| **Mugs / cups** | ~12 | Ceramic mugs, metal/enamel mugs, keep cups |
| **Notebooks** | ~10 | Hardcover notebooks with brand pattern or logo |
| **Pens / pencils** | ~8 | Often as part of a stationery-adjacent set |
| **Hoodies / sweatshirts** | ~8 | Premium merchandise item in lifestyle and tech brands |
| **Water bottles** | ~6 | SIGG-style bottles, often in primary brand color |
| **Badges / pins** | ~5 | Enamel pins as collectibles (CORWIN, Vratna) |
| **Stickers** | ~7 | Often in multiple formats (rectangle, circle, die-cut). Tramways Coffee (E083) and Edwin Castillo (E087) both feature stickers as core brand applications |
| **Socks** | ~2 | Vratna (D017) |
| **Umbrellas** | ~1 | NOVEBA (D033) |

### Industry patterns

Merchandise coverage correlates strongly with industry:

- **Real estate developers** (CORWIN, NOVEBA, Vratna): Extensive merchandise including construction-site items (helmets, reflective vests, badges) alongside lifestyle items (honey, keep cups, plantable pencils). CORWIN's merchandise section is one of the most imaginative -- it includes enamel pins for each of the brand's six principles and urban honey ("MESTSKY MED") as a community product.
- **Tech / SaaS**: Light on merchandise. Often limited to a single page showing a t-shirt or cap. Digital-first brands deprioritize physical goods.
- **Events / festivals**: Focused merchandise (BJD jazz festival: t-shirts, wristbands; GameJam: none).
- **Cultural institutions / museums**: Rarely include merchandise guidelines, preferring to focus on signage and publications.
- **Sports / lifestyle**: Extensive but often focused on licensee control rather than design templates (Umbro, Nike Empower, ASICS).

### The NOVEBA standard

D033-NOVEBA sets the benchmark for merchandise coverage in the Diorama portfolio: envelopes, keychains, notebooks, metal pens, hoodies, t-shirts, polo shirts, dress shirts, winter caps, umbrellas, canvas bags, helmets, reflective vests, and badges/patches. This reflects the practical reality that real estate agency staff visit construction sites, attend client meetings, and distribute promotional items across a wide range of physical contexts.

## 8. Presentation Templates

### Prevalence

Presentation templates (PowerPoint/Keynote slide decks) appear in ~45 documents (26%). This makes them the sixth most common application type -- remarkably high given that presentations were rarely addressed in brand guidelines before 2015.

### What is included

Documents that address presentations typically provide:

- **Title slide** with brand lockup and project/client fields
- **Section divider** slides
- **Content slides** with text hierarchy (heading, body, bullet points)
- **Photo/image slides** with overlay treatments
- **Chart/data slides** with branded data visualization colors
- **Closing/contact slide**

The most thorough examples:

- **FONA (D003)**: 8 pages covering title slides, section headers, photo overlays, content slides, list slides, full-bleed dividers, and closing slides. 1920x1080px specifications.
- **SHARK (D002)**: Light/dark variants for different projection environments (light for office screens, dark for projectors).
- **IBM Garage (E063)**: Full slide deck system with case study, table, map, and fact layouts.
- **Origameo (E003)**: PowerPoint and Word templates documented alongside letterhead.
- **Research Ireland (E141)**: Covers, breakers, and content slides with editable templates available on request.
- **HSBC (E082)**: Presentation templates within a broader literature and digital section.

### The trend

Presentation templates are growing faster than any other application type. In the Diorama portfolio specifically, they appear consistently from D003-FONA (2018) onward. This reflects the reality that for many organizations -- particularly B2B, corporate, and institutional brands -- the slide deck is the most frequently produced branded asset, far exceeding business cards or letterhead in daily usage volume.

## 9. Best Examples -- Top 5 Application Sections

The following five documents represent the strongest application sections in the entire corpus, evaluated on breadth of touchpoints, specificity of specifications, real-world practicality, and craft quality.

### 1. D017-Vratna (5/5) -- The Resort Standard

60 pages of applications spanning: stationery (business cards, folder, email signature, gift vouchers), on-site branding (ski passes, branded coffee cups, restaurant menus with real pricing, tourist/ski maps), wayfinding (notice boards, warning signs, facility signage, directional signs with distance markers), vehicle wraps (three specific vehicle models), OOH (citylights, billboards in multiple variants), social media (Instagram Stories, Reels, feed grid, Highlights covers), and merchandise (mugs, beanies, socks, hoodies, tote bags, badges). Every application uses real data -- actual menu items, staff names, vehicle models, GPS coordinates. This is a working production document, not a conceptual exercise.

### 2. HSBC (E082, 5/5) -- The Enterprise Standard

181 pages covering: literature (A4/A5/DL brochure covers and spreads with grid system, fund brochures, newsletters, factsheets, whitepapers), stationery (letterhead in A4 and US formats, continuation sheets, compliments slips, business cards, DL/C4/C5 envelopes, folders, global cards), digital (eNewsletter, email footer), events (eInvitations, print invitations, corporate backdrops, stage dressing, merchandise), and an extraordinarily detailed app icon system (core banking apps, sub-brand apps, product/utility apps, internal apps, watch apps, social icons, app store best practices). Version-controlled with multilingual support.

### 3. NJ Transit (E065, 5/5) -- The Infrastructure Standard

169 pages covering every conceivable transit touchpoint: vehicles (specific bus models by manufacturer, locomotives, push-pull cars, rail cars, corporate sedans, vans, tow-wreckers), signage (bus stops in six configurations, trailblazers, bus terminal identification, rail station signage from vehicular entrance through platform and track crossing), schedules, maps (geographic and schematic), and corporate forms. Material specifications include aluminum panels, steel posts, paint, screen printing, decals, and reflective sheeting. The legibility formula (1" cap height per 50 feet viewing distance) is unmatched.

### 4. D016-CORWIN (4/5) -- The Real Estate Standard

50+ pages of applications across: print (citylights, billboards, brochures, annual reports, document templates, event popup walls, banners, roll-ups, posters), digital (slide decks, social media grids), stationery (business cards on kraft paper, DL and C5 envelopes, letterhead, folders with principle symbols, notebooks), and an exceptionally imaginative merchandise section (t-shirts, hoodies, caps, beanies, tote bags, plantable pencils, water bottles, keep cups, enamel principle pins, urban honey). The kraft paper material choice reinforces the sustainability brand positioning through physical touchpoints.

### 5. F1 (E060, 5/5) -- The Sports Standard

205 pages covering: press releases, website, event lockups, partner lockups, official merchandise, driver biography pages, fan festival communications, OOH, digital, print, and environmental applications. Detailed schematic examples for logo placement across all applications. Vinyl and RAL specifications for physical applications. Three logo size variants (Standard, Display, Micro) with different artwork -- not just scaled versions. The combination of strategic depth with production-level detail across both digital and physical touchpoints is exemplary.

### Honorable mentions

- **D033-NOVEBA** (4.5/5): 82 pages including OOH, print, digital, and the most extensive construction-site merchandise set (helmets, reflective vests) in the corpus.
- **AB-InBev (E075)** (5/5): 192 pages with a "Flex Assets" modular system and extensive application demonstrations.
- **Burger King (E135)** (4/5): Covers packaging, menuboards, restaurant signage, social media, digital (including branded QR codes), OOH, swag, and television. Food-as-design-language philosophy runs through every application.
- **Dreher (E129)** (4/5): Historically significant mid-century Italian manual with engineering-precision vehicle livery drawings for specific Fiat models and beer glass construction drawings. A masterclass in treating brand identity as industrial design.

## 10. Toolkit Implications -- Which Applications to Generate by Default

Based on the frequency analysis, quality correlation data, and temporal trends across the corpus, the following recommendations emerge for a brand guidelines generation toolkit.

### Tier 1: Always generate (present in 40%+ of documents; expected by clients)

| Application | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **Business cards** | Most common application (56%). Universal expectation. Low effort, high perceived value. Include front/back with real contact information. |
| **Social media templates** | Second most common (46%) and the fastest-growing category. Instagram feed (1080x1080) and Story (1080x1920) as minimum. LinkedIn post as secondary. |
| **Letterhead** | Third most common (42%). Single A4 page with logo, contact, and text zone. Include email signature as the digital equivalent. |
| **Poster / OOH** | Fourth most common (40%). A single portrait-format poster or citylight demonstrates the brand at scale. |

### Tier 2: Generate by default with option to exclude (present in 20--35%)

| Application | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **Website hero section** | Present in 32% but clients increasingly expect digital-first proof of concept. A single homepage hero mockup suffices. |
| **Presentation template** | Present in 26% and growing fast. A title slide, content slide, and section divider cover the essentials. Critical for B2B/corporate clients. |
| **Email signature** | Present in 24%. Low effort to generate; high daily usage value for clients. Include name, title, logo, and contact fields. |
| **Merchandise (t-shirt)** | Present in 22%. A single t-shirt mockup demonstrates the brand on physical goods. Expand to additional items for retail/lifestyle/real estate clients. |

### Tier 3: Generate when industry-relevant (present in 10--20%)

| Application | When to include |
|---|---|
| **Signage / wayfinding** | Real estate, hospitality, transit, museums, retail |
| **Folder / document cover** | Real estate, finance, corporate, institutional |
| **Digital ads / banners** | E-commerce, SaaS, media brands |
| **Envelope (DL)** | Corporate, institutional, luxury -- omit for digital-first brands |
| **Vehicle wrap** | Infrastructure, delivery, resort, transit |
| **Roll-up banner** | Events, B2B, trade show contexts |

### Tier 4: Generate on request only (present in <10%)

| Application | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Packaging** | Highly product-specific. Only for CPG, food, beverage brands. Tramways Coffee (E083) demonstrates tiered packaging (150g/250g/1kg) with sticker system integration |
| **App icon** | Only for brands with mobile products |
| **Video / motion templates** | Resource-intensive; only for broadcast, streaming, or video-first brands |
| **Newsletter / email** | Only when the client has an active email marketing program |
| **Uniforms / workwear** | Only for hospitality, construction, transit |
| **Exhibition / trade show** | Only for B2B brands with events programs |

### Key design decisions for the toolkit

1. **Lead with digital, include print.** The modern application section should open with social media and website, then move to stationery and print. This mirrors the actual production priority for most brands.

2. **Use real data in mockups.** The strongest application sections in the corpus (Vratna, CORWIN, NOVEBA) use real names, real addresses, real pricing, and real content. Placeholder "Lorem ipsum" mockups feel unfinished and reduce perceived quality.

3. **Show context, not just layouts.** Environmental mockups (business cards on a desk surface, posters in a street setting, social media on a phone screen) significantly increase perceived value over flat layout presentations.

4. **Include an email signature.** This tiny application is disproportionately valuable to clients. It is used daily, requires pixel-precise specification, and is easy to generate. Many of the strongest documents in the corpus (SHARK, FONA, HSBC, ASICS, Aludium) give email signatures a full page of specs.

5. **Scale merchandise to industry.** A tech startup needs one t-shirt mockup. A real estate developer needs helmets, reflective vests, and construction-site signage. A resort needs ski passes, branded cups, and trail markers. The toolkit should offer industry-aware merchandise expansion.

6. **Presentation templates are now essential.** The slide deck has replaced letterhead as the highest-frequency branded asset for most organizations. Any toolkit that omits presentation templates is missing the application that clients will use most often.
