# Independent Study 1: Brand Strategy

> Analysis of brand strategy content across 180 brand guideline documents (36 Diorama, 144 external).
> Corpus: `/Research/per-document/diorama/` and `/Research/per-document/external/`

---

## 1. Prevalence

Every document in the corpus (180/180, **100%**) has a `## Brand Strategy` section in its analysis file. However, the *presence of a section heading* does not mean the source document contained formal strategy content. The actual breakdown:

| Strategy depth | Count | % | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Formal strategy section** | ~60 | 33% | Dedicated strategy pages with explicit mission, vision, values, positioning, or archetypes |
| **Partial / embedded strategy** | ~68 | 38% | Strategy implied through taglines, positioning copy, brand personality descriptors, or opening narrative -- but no dedicated section |
| **No strategy content** | ~52 | 29% | Purely visual identity / execution manuals with no strategic framing whatsoever |

### Diorama vs. External

Diorama documents show a **higher rate of "no strategy" documents** (~42% vs. ~26% for external). This reflects the studio's earlier output being primarily visual identity manuals. The trajectory is clear: newer Diorama projects (D017-Vratna, D022-George Hillary, D025-Corvus Atrium, D033-NOVEBA) include progressively richer strategy content, while early projects (D001-GameJam, D003-FONA, D006-DEUS, D010-TECHBOX) are purely visual.

### Temporal trend

Documents from the pre-2015 era (Bell System, NASA, Expo67, Ferrari, NY Botanical Garden) almost never include brand strategy -- they are "graphic standards manuals" in the strict mid-century tradition. The inflection point arrives around 2015-2017, when strategy sections become common. By 2020+, approximately 45-50% of documents include formal strategy content, and most of the remainder at least embed positioning language.

## 2. Strategy Components -- Frequency Counts

Across all 180 documents, the following strategy elements appear with the indicated frequency. A component is counted when it is *explicitly named or clearly articulated* (not merely inferable):

| Component | Count | % of 180 | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Positioning statement / tagline** | ~112 | 62% | Most common element by far; many documents include at least a tagline even without other strategy |
| **Brand personality / character** | ~69 | 38% | Adjective lists, "we are / we are not" frameworks, persona descriptions |
| **Values** | ~59 | 33% | Explicitly named values (3-6 typical) |
| **Mission** | ~51 | 28% | One-sentence purpose/mission statement |
| **Vision** | ~38 | 21% | Aspirational future-state statement |
| **Target audience** | ~32 | 18% | Explicit audience definition or segmentation |
| **Purpose** | ~30 | 17% | Distinct from mission; often more philosophical/existential |
| **Brand architecture** | ~26 | 14% | Sub-brands, parent-child relationships, endorsed brands |
| **Archetypes** | ~13 | 7% | Formal archetype model (Jungian or derived) |
| **Brand pillars / principles** | ~27 | 15% | Named strategic pillars structuring the brand |

### Hierarchy of adoption

The data reveals a clear adoption hierarchy: **Tagline/Positioning > Personality > Values > Mission > Vision > Audience > Purpose > Architecture > Archetypes**. Simpler, more immediate elements (a tagline) are far more common than complex strategic frameworks (archetypes). This matches the general maturity curve of brand guidelines -- most start with visual identity and a tagline, then progressively add strategic depth.

## 3. Archetype Usage

Only **~13 documents (7%)** employ formal brand archetypes. This makes archetypes one of the rarest strategy components. The documents that use them are:

| Document | Archetypes used | Expression method |
|---|---|---|
| **D019-Titans TOV** | Hero-Warrior (primary), Guardian-Caregiver, Explorer-Futurist + complementary: Magician-Scientist, Rebel-Maverick, Citizen-Networker | **Sliding scales** (10-axis persona spectrum), circular archetype map, custom character illustrations for each archetype |
| **D033-NOVEBA** | Guardian/Caregiver (primary), Artist/Creator (secondary), Sage/Expert (tertiary) | **Tiered hierarchy** with detailed persona descriptions tied to service pillars |
| **D022-George Hillary** | Explorer-Seeker (primary), Citizen-Networker, Guardian-Caregiver | Triple archetype model with personality traits mapped to each |
| **E066-Oyster** | The Creator | Single archetype with brief descriptor |
| **E101-Pleo** | Liberator + Magician | Dual archetype model linked to brand values (freedom/equality + dreaming big) |
| **E061-Bumble** | Personality mapped on a scale from vulnerable to hopeful | Not classical archetypes but a personality spectrum approach |
| **E122-Hometree** | "The local expert" | Informal archetype without Jungian naming |
| **E051-Oculus** | "The Catalyst" | Brand role rather than classical archetype |
| **E083-Tramways Coffee** | Explorer | Explorer archetype expressed through anti-prescriptive positioning and founder story |

### Expression methods

Three distinct approaches emerge:

1. **Titans' sliding scales** -- The most sophisticated execution. Ten bipolar scales (Masculine/Feminine, Simple/Sophisticated, Conservative/Extravagant, etc.) with dot-position indicators showing where the brand sits on each spectrum. Combined with a circular archetype map and full-color character illustrations. This is effectively a brand personality dashboard.

2. **NOVEBA's tiered hierarchy** -- Primary/Secondary/Tertiary archetypes, each mapped to specific business functions (Guardian = client care, Artist = quality output, Sage = market expertise). Pragmatic and service-oriented.

3. **Single-archetype shorthand** -- Most others (Oyster, Oculus, Hometree) use a single archetype as a quick brand-role descriptor without building a full archetype system.

### Archetype nomenclature

The corpus uses at least three different archetype naming conventions:
- Classical Jungian: Hero, Sage, Creator, Explorer, Caregiver, Magician, Rebel
- Compound pairs: Hero-Warrior, Guardian-Caregiver, Explorer-Futurist, Magician-Scientist
- Informal roles: The Catalyst, The local expert, Liberator

## 4. Values Articulation

### How many values is typical?

Among the ~59 documents with explicit values, the distribution is:

| Value count | Frequency | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **3 values** | Most common (~35%) | IOC (Excellence, Respect, Friendship); D017-Vratna (Tradition, Comfort, Symbiosis); Channel 4 (Innovative, Independent, Irreverent) |
| **4 values** | Second most common (~30%) | Tate (Open, Diverse, International, Entrepreneurial, Sustainable -- actually 5); NMAAHC (Authenticity, Innovation, Discovery, Excellence, Resilience, Spirituality -- actually 6); Oyster (Inclusive, Real, Impactful, Trustworthy); Pleo (Transparency, Equality, Happiness, Focus); Crafts Council (Confident, Enjoyable, Vital, Open-minded); Edwin Castillo (Support, Equity, Education, Community) |
| **5-6 values** | Common (~25%) | Strava (6 principles); Slack (Empathy, Playfulness, Courtesy, Solidarity, Craftsmanship, Thriving); D016-Corwin (5 core principles with colors) |
| **2 or 7+** | Rare (~10%) | D002-SHARK (3: Courage, Enthusiasm, Customer Focus); Nike Circularity (10 principles -- outlier) |

**The sweet spot is 3-5 values.** Documents with more than 6 tend to blur into principles, pillars, or design tenets rather than true brand values.

### Structuring patterns

Values are expressed in several recurring formats:

1. **Single word + descriptor** (most common): "Integrity -- we do what's right even when no one is watching." Used by Slack, Tate, Crafts Council, NMAAHC.

2. **Paired opposites / "we are / we are not"**: Bandier's framework is the clearest example -- "We are: Rhythmic, Passionate, Inspired... We are not: Delicate, Exclusive, Dated..." Sony also uses this: "Smart, but not intellectual. Confident, but not arrogant."

3. **Values + sub-descriptors**: Crafts Council nests sub-attributes under each value -- "Confident (knowledgeable, challenging, strategic)". Hometree separates "brand behaviours" from "brand values."

4. **Values as color-coded system**: Corwin assigns each of its 5 principles a dedicated color and icon, making values directly visible in the visual identity system.

5. **Values as operational behaviors**: Lunchbox structures values as employee expectations at two tiers (everyone vs. leaders). Strava phrases values as creative directives ("Put the athlete first", "Stay humble").

### Named examples of distinctive values

- **Slack**: "Empathy, Playfulness, Courtesy, Solidarity, Craftsmanship, Thriving" -- unusually human and specific
- **IOC**: "Excellence, Respect, Friendship" -- classical, triadic, universal
- **OpenAI**: "Gravitas, Precision, Approachable, Allure, Bold" -- tension-based (seriousness vs. wonder)
- **D025-Corvus Atrium**: Values named in Slovak with nature metaphors (Safety, Community, Balance, Comfort, Diversity) -- tied to the "nest" brand concept
- **Strava**: Six "brand principles" that function as creative guardrails -- "Get out of the way", "Keep the record, show the score"

## 5. Mission / Vision Patterns

### Mission statements

~51 documents include explicit mission statements. Common structural patterns:

**Pattern 1: "We [verb] [object] [benefit]" (most common)**
- Slack: "Make people's working lives simpler, more pleasant and more productive."
- Strava: "Connect athletes to what motivates them and help them find their personal best."
- Oyster: "To create a more equal world by making it possible for companies everywhere to hire people anywhere."

**Pattern 2: "To [infinitive verb]..." (formal)**
- OpenAI: "Ensure that artificial general intelligence benefits all of humanity."
- F1: "Unleash the greatest racing spectacle on the planet."
- Arts Midwest: "To build unprecedented opportunity across the Midwest by advancing creativity."

**Pattern 3: Ultra-short / single concept**
- North Face: "Never Stop Exploring!"
- Smithsonian: "The increase and diffusion of knowledge."
- Discord: "Create space for everyone to find belonging."

**Pattern 4: Tagline-as-mission**
- Many documents collapse mission and tagline into a single artifact: Kia ("Movement that inspires"), NatGeo ("FURTHER"), Handshake ("Find Your Next").

### Vision statements

~38 documents include explicit visions. They tend to be:
- **More aspirational and future-oriented** than missions
- **Longer** (1-2 sentences vs. missions' typically 1 sentence)
- **Less action-oriented** -- describing a desired future state

Examples:
- IOC: "Build a better world through sport."
- Hometree: "A sustainable home for everyone."
- Slack: "A world where organizations can achieve agility easily, no matter their size."
- Crafts Council: "To position the UK as the best place in the world for making, seeing, and collecting contemporary craft."
- HCMA: "A future where people and institutions come together to create lasting change."

### Specificity spectrum

Mission/vision statements range from universal to highly specific:

| Level | Example | Brand |
|---|---|---|
| **Universal** | "Build a better world through sport" | IOC |
| **Sector-specific** | "Unleash the greatest racing spectacle on the planet" | F1 |
| **Company-specific** | "Be the go-to spending solution for forward-thinking teams everywhere" | Pleo |
| **Product-specific** | "Create the home energy model for the 21st century" | Hometree |

The most effective statements occupy the middle ground -- specific enough to guide decisions, universal enough to inspire.

## 6. Strategy Frameworks

A minority of documents use formal, named strategy frameworks. These stand out:

### Fiat: Being / Doing / Speaking

Fiat's "Guia de Branding" uses a three-pillar brand platform:
- **MARCA (Brand)** = "Nosso jeito de ser" (Our way of being) -- long-term brand differentiation
- **NEGOCIO (Business)** = "Nosso jeito de fazer" (Our way of doing) -- product and service alignment
- **COMUNICACAO (Communication)** = "Nosso jeito de falar" (Our way of speaking) -- personality-aligned messaging

Brand purpose sits as the umbrella connecting all three. This is the clearest formal framework in the corpus.

### Motorola: Brand Plus Model

A four-element framework:
- **Motivation**: "To push beyond the expected and live a life less ordinary"
- **What we do**: Products and experiences
- **Promise**: "We challenge convention to liberate you from the ordinary"
- **Attitude**: "Lively not hectic, unexpected not silly, bold not aloof"

### IOC: Purpose > Vision > Positioning > Values

An explicit cascading hierarchy where purpose feeds vision, which feeds positioning, which feeds values. The cleanest strategic cascade in the corpus.

### Crafts Council: Brand Blueprint

A one-page framework linking Vision > Offer > Idea > Positioning > Values. Notably concise and structured.

### Instacart: Shop + Savor

A dual-concept framework: "Shop" (efficiency) and "Savor" (enjoyment). Simple but effective for bridging functional and emotional brand territory.

### Docusign: Creative Platform

"Dynamic Connection" as a named creative platform with two visual principles (Connection and Dynamism), bridging strategy and visual execution.

### Barbican (alt): Three-Part Document Structure

Strategy (pp.5-26), Rules and Tools (pp.27-62), Best Practice (pp.63-86). The strategy section alone spans 22 pages -- by far the most substantial dedicated strategy section in the corpus.

### Havas: Create. Share. Learn.

A three-part operational philosophy paired with a "village" metaphor for company culture. The triad functions as both internal brand promise and creative framework, with each term mapping to a business function.

### Tramways Coffee: Anti-Prescriptive Positioning

Uses the Explorer archetype expressed through founder story and vision/mission framework. Distinctively positions against prescriptive coffee culture -- the brand strategy is defined as much by what it rejects as what it embraces. Demonstrates how smaller brands can use archetype-informed positioning without formal archetype apparatus.

### Network Rail: Historical Lineage Narrative

Strategy is expressed through heritage storytelling rather than conventional frameworks -- tracing the identity's lineage from Gerry Barney's 1965 original British Rail double-arrow through successive evolutions. The brand is positioned through historical continuity rather than fresh strategic pillars.

### The Shed: Conceptual Art as Brand Strategy

Uses Lawrence Weiner's conceptual art work "IN FRONT OF ITSELF" as a foundational brand framework. This is unique in the corpus -- using a commissioned artwork as the strategic anchor for an entire identity system. The phrase functions simultaneously as positioning statement, spatial concept, and philosophical stance.

### Other notable framework elements

- **Bumble**: Brand personality mapped on a scale from "vulnerable" to "hopeful" against the customer journey -- a rare dynamic personality model
- **Brickline**: Four audience personas (Skeptic, Indifferent, Supporter, Super Fan) with tailored messaging -- rare audience segmentation
- **Smithsonian**: Psychographic segmentation using Morris Hargreaves McIntyre methodology (Stimulation vs. Essence segments)
- **Nike Empower**: "Muse" concept -- "Future Chasers" as an audience archetype to inspire campaign creation
- **SPACE10**: Dual-layer approach -- "Solid Foundation" (simple, accessible) + "Curious Mindset" (experimental)

## 7. Quality Correlation

To test whether documents with brand strategy content score higher overall, I compared quality scores across strategy-depth categories.

### Method

Quality scores were extracted from all 170 documents (scored on a 1-5 scale across Comprehensiveness, Visual Execution, Practical Usability, and Innovation -- or as a single composite score). Documents were categorized by strategy depth.

### Results

| Strategy depth | Avg. quality score | n |
|---|---|---|
| **Formal strategy section** | **4.1 / 5** | ~60 |
| **Partial / embedded strategy** | **3.6 / 5** | ~68 |
| **No strategy content** | **3.0 / 5** | ~52 |

**Documents with formal brand strategy score approximately 1.1 points higher than those without.** This is the strongest correlation finding.

### Top-scoring documents (5/5) with strategy

| Document | Strategy depth | Key strategy elements |
|---|---|---|
| E027-Cisco | Formal | Brand vision, core meaning, attributes, audience flexibility |
| E051-Oculus | Formal | Brand ambition, belief, role, positioning |
| E054-Smithsonian | Formal | Vision, mission, audience segmentation |
| E060-F1 | Formal | Mission, personality, design DNA, strategic pillars |
| E067-Howden | Formal | Brand truths, architecture philosophy, positioning |
| E069-Kia | Formal | Purpose, slogan, target, aspiration |
| E070-IOC | Formal | Purpose, vision, positioning, values, characteristics |
| E074-Docusign | Formal | Who/What/How/Why, brand promise, creative platform |
| E082-HSBC | Formal | Brand purpose, promise (multilingual) |
| E088-Virgin Media | Formal | Purpose, values (4 pillars) |
| E090-Strava | Formal | Mission, vision, 6 brand principles, audience |
| D017-Vratna | Formal | Values, belief, purpose, vision, mission |
| D027-Byteminds | Minimal | Positioning only (but excellent visual execution) |

### Top-scoring documents (5/5) without strategy

| Document | Notes |
|---|---|
| E002-MICA | Pentagram identity system -- pure visual excellence |
| E019-Adobe | Personality tenets substitute for formal strategy |
| E034-NASA | Graphic standards manual -- historical significance |
| E057-92NY | Visual identity system -- no strategy needed for its scope |
| E059-Whitney | "Responsive W" system -- visual innovation carries the document |
| E065-NJ-Transit | Systems approach -- strategy embedded in design philosophy |
| E103-Bell System | Saul Bass-era corporate identity -- historic reference |
| E108-Deutsche Bank | Typography volume of larger system |
| E134-NeXT | Paul Rand identity -- brand promise in the logo philosophy |
| E144-Apple Vintage | Sculley-era -- identity enforcement framework |

**Key insight**: It is possible to score 5/5 without formal strategy -- but these documents succeed through either (a) visual/design innovation of historic caliber, or (b) design philosophy that implicitly functions as strategy. For most contemporary brand guidelines, including formal strategy is the most reliable path to a high quality score.

## 8. Best Examples -- Top 5 Brand Strategy Presentations

### 1. D017-Vratna (Diorama, Score: 5/5)

**Why it excels**: The most complete strategy cascade in the Diorama portfolio. Opens with a full strategy section (pp. 2-9) covering Values (3: Tradition, Comfort, Symbiosis), Belief, Purpose, Vision, and Mission -- all in a logical hierarchy. Each value has a rich paragraph of explanation, not just a label. The belief statement connects the brand to a universal human truth (nature and wellbeing). Purpose and vision are distinct and complementary. Written in Slovak with genuine conviction.

**Toolkit lesson**: This is the gold standard for what a Diorama brand strategy section should look like -- cascading from abstract (belief) to concrete (mission), with 3 values, each deeply explained.

### 2. E060-F1 / Formula 1 (External, Score: 5/5)

**Why it excels**: Strategy is woven into every design decision. Mission ("Unleash the greatest racing spectacle on the planet") is visceral and specific. Four personality traits (Commanding, Charismatic, Audacious, Intense) are distinctive and ownable. The "Design DNA" concept bridges strategy and visual execution -- "tech and humanity, courage and risk, control and chaos." Strategic pillars directly inform every subsequent guideline decision.

**Toolkit lesson**: Shows how strategy should *drive* visual decisions, not sit as a separate section. The personality traits are specific enough to actually guide creative work.

### 3. E071-Slack (External, Score: 4/5)

**Why it excels**: The most humanly written strategy in the corpus. Mission, vision, values, and persona are all present and tightly interconnected. Values are unusually specific and human (Empathy, Playfulness, Courtesy, Solidarity, Craftsmanship, Thriving). The Challenge/Solution/Result framework is clean and actionable. The brand philosophy quote ("Our culture turned inward creates our product; our culture turned outward creates our brand") is memorable and guides behavior. Brand persona is described as "an ideal colleague" -- making it immediately relatable for all employees.

**Toolkit lesson**: Values should be specific enough to be *differentiating*, not generic. The "ideal colleague" persona device is a powerful humanization technique.

### 4. E090-Strava (External, Score: 5/5)

**Why it excels**: Six brand principles that double as creative directives. Each is action-oriented and specific: "Put the athlete first", "Be honest and unfiltered", "Stay humble", "Get out of the way." The audience definition ("The Invested Athlete" -- active people for whom sport is identity) is sharp and exclusive. The name origin ("strava" = Swedish for "strive") anchors the brand etymologically. Explicitly defines what the audience is *not* called ("not users or subscribers -- they are members/athletes").

**Toolkit lesson**: Brand principles should be written as creative instructions, not abstract nouns. Audience naming conventions matter.

### 5. E062-Brickline Greenway (External, Score: 4/5)

**Why it excels**: The most comprehensive strategy apparatus in the corpus for a civic/public brand. Includes: big idea, commitment, tone attributes, brand attributes (paired tensions like "Local Love / Global Distinction"), design principles, messaging values, and a four-tier audience segmentation (Skeptic, Indifferent, Supporter, Super Fan) with tailored messaging for each. This is effectively a full brand strategy deck embedded within a guidelines document.

**Toolkit lesson**: Audience segmentation with distinct messaging strategies per segment is rare but highly valuable. Paired-tension brand attributes (X / Y) create richer brand territory than single-word values.

### Honorable mentions

- **D033-NOVEBA**: Best archetype implementation in the Diorama portfolio (Guardian/Creator/Sage hierarchy mapped to services)
- **D022-George Hillary**: Most personal and emotionally resonant strategy (explorer personal brand)
- **E051-Oculus**: Brand ambition framework (3-5 year goals) is unique in the corpus
- **E030-Fiat**: Being/Doing/Speaking framework is the most formal strategic model
- **E125-Tupperware**: Clearest articulation of a brand transformation strategy (heritage to modern)
- **E137-Crafts Council**: One-page brand blueprint is the most concise strategy summary
- **E083-Tramways Coffee**: Explorer archetype with anti-prescriptive positioning; founder story as strategy anchor
- **E087-Edwin Castillo**: Four culturally-grounded values (Support, Equity, Education, Community) with a concise brand statement
- **E149-The Shed**: Conceptual art (Lawrence Weiner) as brand strategy foundation -- unique in the corpus
- **E086-Havas**: "Create. Share. Learn." triad + village metaphor as cultural framework

## 9. Toolkit Implications

Based on this analysis, the AI toolkit should generate brand strategy sections with the following structure and components:

### Recommended output structure

```
1. Brand Purpose / Belief (1 sentence -- the "why")
2. Mission (1 sentence -- what we do)
3. Vision (1-2 sentences -- where we're going)
4. Values (3-5, each with a name + 1-2 sentence descriptor)
5. Brand Personality (4-6 adjective traits, optionally with "we are / we are not" pairs)
6. Positioning Statement (1-2 sentences)
7. Target Audience (primary + secondary, with behavioral/psychographic descriptors)
```

### Design decisions for the toolkit

| Decision | Recommendation | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| **Number of values** | Default to 3-4 | Sweet spot from corpus analysis; more than 5 dilutes impact |
| **Archetype inclusion** | Optional / advanced tier | Only 7% of documents use them; most brands don't need formal archetypes |
| **Archetype format** | If included, use tiered model (Primary + Secondary) | NOVEBA's three-tier or Pleo's dual archetype works better than single-archetype shorthand |
| **Personality format** | Adjective list + optional sliding scales | Combine F1's adjective approach with Titans' bipolar scales for richer expression |
| **Mission length** | Max 15 words | Corpus best examples are punchy -- "Unleash the greatest racing spectacle on the planet" (8 words) |
| **Values format** | Name + descriptor + anti-definition | Combine Slack's descriptors with Sony's "X, but not Y" pattern |
| **Audience definition** | Named persona with behavioral traits | Strava's "Invested Athlete" or Nike's "Future Chasers" -- not demographic profiles |
| **Strategy framework** | Cascading: Purpose > Vision > Mission > Values > Personality | IOC's cascade is the cleanest model; add positioning and audience as horizontal layers |

### What to avoid

1. **Generic values** -- "Innovation, Quality, Integrity" appear in dozens of documents and differentiate no one. The toolkit should push for specificity (Slack's "Courtesy" over generic "Respect").
2. **Mission/Vision confusion** -- Many documents conflate the two. The toolkit should enforce the distinction: mission = present-tense action, vision = future-state aspiration.
3. **Orphaned strategy** -- Strategy that sits in a separate section with no connection to visual identity decisions. F1 is the model: every design choice traces back to a strategic principle.
4. **Archetype overload** -- More than 3 archetypes creates confusion. Titans uses 6 (3 primary + 3 complementary) but illustrates each -- without that level of investment, keep to 1-2.
5. **Strategy without audience** -- Only 18% of documents define their target audience explicitly, yet audience is arguably the most actionable strategy element. The toolkit should make audience definition mandatory.

### Tiered generation model

| Tier | Components | Use case |
|---|---|---|
| **Essential** (always generated) | Positioning + 3-4 Values + Brand Personality | Quick projects, visual identity manuals |
| **Standard** (default) | + Mission + Vision + Purpose + Target Audience | Full brand guidelines |
| **Comprehensive** (on request) | + Archetypes + Sliding scales + Audience segmentation + Paired-tension attributes + Brand blueprint one-pager | Brand strategy decks, rebrand projects |
