# Layer 2 — Role and message

What is this deck arguing, and what should each slide make someone feel?

---

## Start with the argument, not the outline

An outline is a list of topics. An argument is a claim that needs proving. Decks built from outlines are complete and forgettable; decks built from arguments are shorter and land.

Two questions, answered in plain language before any slide exists:

1. **What does this audience need to believe by the end?**
2. **What would make them believe it?**

Everything in the deck is either the claim, evidence for the claim, or the ask. Anything that is none of those is a slide you have not deleted yet.

---

## The headline spine

Write every slide's headline as a flat list, in order. Read the list on its own.

**If the headlines alone do not make the argument, no layout will rescue the deck.** Fix the spine before designing. This takes twenty minutes and saves a day.

A good spine reads as consecutive sentences:

> Awareness was never the problem → The problem is conviction → Here is what conviction looks like when we build it → Here it is again, in a different category → Here is how we would do it for you → Here is what it costs → Here is the next step

A bad spine reads as a table of contents:

> Introduction → About us → Our work → Our process → Scope → Pricing → Thank you

---

## One slide, one job

Each slide does exactly one of these. Name it before designing it.

| Role | What it does | Typical archetype |
|---|---|---|
| **Assert** | States the claim, unqualified | STATEMENT |
| **Prove** | Shows work that makes the claim credible | SPLIT, GALLERY |
| **Quantify** | Converts the claim into a number | STACK (stat) |
| **Explain** | Sets out how something works | STACK, STATEMENT dense |
| **Orient** | Tells the audience where they are | Contents, divider |
| **Ask** | States the next action | STATEMENT |

A slide doing two jobs should be two slides. A slide doing none should be cut.

---

## Emotion register

Decks fail more often from uniform tone than from wrong tone. Decide what each slide should feel like, and let that drive scale and density rather than decoration.

| Register | Feels like | How it is made |
|---|---|---|
| **Assured** | Someone who has done this before | Large type, deep void, few elements |
| **Rigorous** | Someone who has counted | Ruled rows, aligned lanes, real numbers |
| **Generous** | Someone showing you something | Big imagery, short captions, air |
| **Candid** | Someone telling you the cost | Plain table, no ornament, no hedging |
| **Warm** | Someone you would work with | Human photography, first person, shorter sentences |

The most common failure is **assured everywhere**. A deck that is entirely large statements has no proof in it, and reads as bluster.

---

## Rhythm across the deck

Sequence is composition across time. Five equally loud slides read as none.

```
LOUD  →  quiet  →  medium  →  DENSE  →  quiet  →  LOUD
```

- A **full-bleed image** is a crescendo.
- A **statement on a dark ground** is a beat.
- A **dense table** is where the audience leans in and reads.
- A **near-empty slide** resets the eye before the next claim.

Practical shape for a pitch: open loud, drop to quiet for the understanding, alternate proof and evidence through the middle, go dense for scope and price, close loud.

Mark the section boundaries with dividers on the dark ground. They are the punctuation.

---

## One silence per slide

Every strong slide has one large deliberate void — usually between the statement and its evidence. It is not leftover space; it is the thing that makes the statement read as considered rather than crowded.

If a slide has no silence, it is over-filled. If it has three, the content is too thin for the frame.

---

## Research earns the argument

Before writing headlines for a client deck, find out what is actually true about them. Public facts — how they are funded, how they launched, what they publish, how big the audience is — turn a generic proposal into an argument that could only have been written for them.

One well-chosen real number does more than a page of positioning language. It also changes what the deck is about: a redesign brief plus "16,470 people paid for this before it existed" becomes a conversion problem, which is a stronger thing to pitch.

Never invent the number. See Layer 3.
