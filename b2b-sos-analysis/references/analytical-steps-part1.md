# Analytical Steps — Part 1 (Steps 0–8)

These are the foundational steps of the SoS analysis. Execute them in strict order before moving to Part 2.

---

## STEP 0 — Interpretation frame & scope

Establish the boundaries of the analysis before touching the data.

**Summarise:**
- The purpose of this SoS analysis
- What decisions it should inform
- What SoS can and cannot reliably say in this case
- That this covers the US market only (other regions later)

**Questions to answer:**
- What strategic decisions should this analysis support?
- What is in scope and what is out of scope?
- Which parts of the interpretation will necessarily be hypotheses?

**Output:** A short "interpretation frame" in 5–10 bullet points.

---

## STEP 1 — Data hygiene

Audit the dataset quality before any interpretation.

**Check:**
- Are brand queries constructed fairly (all relevant variants, product names, abbreviations, misspellings captured)?
- Is the non-brand query universe logically defensible?
- Does it contain obvious noise or ambiguous multi-meaning terms?
- Are brands comparable to each other in how they are measured?
- Is the time series stable enough to read trends?

**Questions to answer:**
- Where could there be methodological distortion?
- Which interpretations are safe and which are risky?
- Are there terms that need cleaning or extra caution?

**Output:** Section titled "Methodological robustness and limits."

---

## STEP 2 — Customer-defined category fit

Examine whether search confirms the category as the customer defines it.

**Analyse:**
- How the market names the problem
- How the market names the solution
- Whether buyers enter the category through the same logic the client assumes
- Where the category bleeds into adjacent or alternative frames

**Questions to answer:**
- In what situation does a buyer actually consider this category?
- What language do they use?
- Does search confirm the client's internal category definition?
- Where does the category mentally split into sub-categories?
- Which alternatives or substitutes appear in the market's mind?

**Output:** "Category fit verdict" covering:
- Confirmed category framing
- Disputed boundaries
- Adjacent categories
- Possible redefinitions

---

## STEP 3 — Competitive field & mental standard

Map competition across three mandatory layers:

1. **Direct competitors** — same category, same solution type
2. **Aspirational competitors** — the client aspires to compete with, often larger or more established
3. **Substitute solutions** — fundamentally different approaches to the same buyer problem

**Identify:**
- Who is the default choice
- Who sets the mental standard for the category
- Who defines the category's language
- Who the client actually competes against in practice

**Questions to answer:**
- Who is the implicit benchmark in search and comparison patterns?
- Which players dominate only at the brand level vs also situationally?
- Which alternative solutions are the real competition in the buyer's mind?

**Output:** "Tiered competitor map" + commentary on what type of competitive game the client is playing.

---

## STEP 4 — Demand structure: brand vs non-brand vs hybrid

Segment demand into:
- **Brand search** — navigational, brand-name queries
- **Non-brand problem search** — pain/problem/trigger queries
- **Non-brand solution/category search** — solution-type or category-label queries
- **Hybrid search** — queries combining brand + problem/solution terms

**Questions to answer:**
- Is the category brand-saturated or open?
- How do people enter demand: via brand, problem, solution, or comparison?
- Where is the client present only navigationally vs already situationally?
- Which hybrid queries indicate existing mental links to the brand?

**Output:** "Demand structure map" with commentary on implications for the category and the client.

---

## STEP 5 — CEP validation & discovery

This is the **core step**. Analyse all relevant query clusters through the CEP lens.

For each candidate CEP or cluster, distinguish:
- **Trigger / situational queries** — the problem or moment that starts the buying journey
- **Solution-framing queries** — how buyers describe the type of solution they want
- **Brand-anchored queries** — queries already linked to a specific brand

**Questions to answer:**
- Which internal CEPs are confirmed in the data?
- Which are not confirmed, weak, or methodologically unreadable?
- What new CEP candidates emerge?
- Is this a genuine category entry point or merely a technical selection criterion?
- In which situations are brands mentally present and where does nobody "own" the situation yet?

**Output:** "CEP evidence map" — a table or structured list with columns:

| CEP | Evidence type | Signal strength | Competition | Brief strategic interpretation |
|-----|---------------|-----------------|-------------|-------------------------------|
| ... | ... | ... | ... | ... |

---

## STEP 6 — CEP prioritisation

Evaluate CEP priority not just by visibility but by **strategic usability**.

For each relevant CEP, assess:
- **Search intensity** — volume and consistency of signal
- **Competitive occupancy** — how crowded the space is
- **Openness** — room for a new entrant
- **Compatibility** — fit with the category and the firm's reality
- **Potential for mental brand linkage** — can the brand credibly own this space?

**Questions to answer:**
- Which CEPs are genuinely top priority?
- Which are latent or secondary?
- Where does it make sense to build the brand long-term?
- Where does it make sense to only capture existing demand?
- Which CEPs already look like NO-GO candidates?

**Output:** "CEP priority grid" with tiers:

| Tier | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Core priority | Build brand presence here |
| Secondary priority | Worth pursuing with less investment |
| Latent | Emerging or under-developed; watch |
| Monitor | Not actionable yet; track over time |
| NO-GO candidate | Avoid — crowded, poor fit, or noise |

---

## STEP 7 — CEP serviceability & availability reality

For each priority CEP, verify whether the firm can realistically serve it.

**Consider:**
- Physical availability (geographic, distribution)
- Digital availability (web presence, findability, self-serve paths)
- Sales model and partner network
- Onboarding experience
- Service and capacity limits
- Ability to explain the offer without a sales conversation
- Presence in shortlists and comparison environments

**Questions to answer:**
- Can the firm serve this CEP today?
- How does a buyer from this CEP actually reach the client?
- What friction blocks service of this space?
- Is the problem in marketing or in availability / delivery reality?

**Output:** "CEP serviceability verdict" for each priority CEP:

| Verdict | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| Serviceable | Firm can deliver today |
| Serviceable with constraints | Possible but with known limitations |
| Not serviceable today | Requires investment or change before activation |

Include main barriers for each.

---

## STEP 8 — Value signals in search

Identify what type of value buyers seek in priority clusters.

Work with four value types:
1. **Functional** — performance, capability, features
2. **Process** — simplicity, speed, ease of implementation
3. **Economic** — cost, ROI, total cost of ownership
4. **Psychological** — trust, risk reduction, confidence, certainty

**Questions to answer:**
- What value type dominates in this cluster?
- Is the language more about performance, simplicity, cost, or assurance?
- Which values recur across CEPs?
- Where is there a mismatch between what the market seeks and what the client communicates?

**Output:** "Value signal map":

| CEP / cluster | Dominant value | Secondary value | Implication |
|---------------|----------------|-----------------|-------------|
| ... | ... | ... | ... |
